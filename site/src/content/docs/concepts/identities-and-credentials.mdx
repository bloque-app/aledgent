## An identity owns ledger accounts

An **identity** is a principal that can own ledger accounts. It is one of three kinds:

| Kind | Is | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `person` | A natural person | |
| `organisation` | A business or another organisation | An integrating party is an organisation |
| `agent` | Autonomous software acting for another identity | Names the identity it acts for, and holds a credential of its own: it never acts under that identity's credential (`REQ-AG-1`) |

An identity is `Active`, `Restricted` or `Closed`. It owns one or more ledger accounts, and whoever holds
authority over an account can act on it ([Custody and authority](../custody-and-authority/)).

**Two facts, two authorities.** aledgent's book is the registry of which identities and ledger accounts
exist (`REQ-ID-7`). Who an identity is as a person or an organisation, and whether it may transact, is
the integrating party's to know. aledgent holds no customer facts, and neither fact can be read from the
other. An integrating party keeps its own record of which identities and accounts serve which of its
users (`REQ-ID-6`).

## Identifiers

Every identifier aledgent mints is `did:`-shaped (`did:aledgent:id:…`, `did:aledgent:acct:…`,
`did:aledgent:op:…`) and comes from a closed registry of kinds, each with a fixed number of parts
(`REQ-ID-1`). An identifier:

- contains no personal data (`INV-17`);
- never changes once minted;
- does not encode how its subject authenticates, which environment it lives in, or anything else that
  can change during its life (`REQ-ID-2`);
- is not a secret. aledgent assumes identifiers leak and relies on none of them for security
  (`REQ-PV-7`).

## Who opens what

| What | Opened by |
| --- | --- |
| An identity | The **identities principal** only: the operator, or the one other principal the operator gives the `open_identity` role (`REQ-ID-6`) |
| A ledger account | The identities principal, for any identity; the identity that will own it, for itself; or a principal holding a delegated grant over that identity that names `open_account` |
| An integrating party, and which principals belong to it | The operator alone (`REQ-ID-7`). A principal belongs to at most one party, and the party is the domain of its client keys |

Nothing creates the first principal from nothing: an integrator's own identity is opened for it by the
deployment. See [Principals and credentials](../../integrate/principals-and-credentials/).

## Credentials are a set

A **credential** is whatever proves that its holder has an authority: a key, a device-bound credential,
an issued secret. Never a name. An identity holds a **set** of credentials, each belonging to that
identity alone (`REQ-AU-20`):

- any credential in the set acts for the identity, unless it is **scoped** to some of its accounts;
- a credential can narrow its own scope and never widen it, or change its own kind (`REQ-AU-4`). This is
  enforced by the service in this release (`INV-22`);
- adding or removing a credential needs an existing credential of the set, or a completed recovery;
- removing the identity's last credential is refused with `ERR-NotAuthorised`: outside a recovery, a
  holder cannot lock themselves out;
- adding or removing one is a command that needs an approval when the caller's proof is a session or a
  signature (`REQ-AU-23`, [Approving a command](../../integrate/approving-a-command/)).

aledgent holds only public material. A secret never appears in a log, a URL, an error or a default
(`REQ-AU-8`).

<Aside type="note" title="Specified, not served by this release">
An identity opened with an empty credential set, and the enrolment that gives it its first credential
(`REQ-ID-8`, `REQ-AU-22`), are specified. This release's ingress does not serve them: every identity is
opened with its first credential's verifier.
</Aside>

## The three schemes

The specification defines a credential by what it proves. The plan binds one **scheme** to each kind,
and every verifier names its scheme (`DEC-19`, built by `F-028`):

| Scheme | Proof of possession | Verifier (public material) | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `key` | A Schnorr signature over Ristretto255 | The 32-byte public key | Custody reclaim proofs and recovery approvals. **Never** a request's authentication |
| `webauthn-es256` | A WebAuthn assertion: ECDSA P-256 with SHA-256 (COSE `-7`) | Credential handle, public key, relying party id, backup eligibility, the sign count at registration | People, with passkeys. A sign-in buys a session |
| `api-key` | A 32-byte secret, presented as it is | The secret's SHA-256 | Services, agents and automation |

**Verification runs at ingress**, where every caller is already authenticated. A passkey sign-in returns
a short-lived session; every sign-in moves the passkey's sign count in ingress's durable store, so a
replayed or cloned authenticator's count is refused on any instance. An API key is presented as it is.
The core checks, on every command and query, that the credential is still an active member of its
identity's set and covers the account, so removing a credential ends its sessions at once. The core
checks a verifier's shape when it joins a set, and verifies `key` proofs itself.

**Relying parties are configuration.** Each deployment lists the relying party ids and origins it
accepts. Only a passkey whose relying party is in that list can be registered, signed in with or imported.

How an integrator holds and presents each one: [Principals and credentials](../../integrate/principals-and-credentials/)
and [API keys from zero](../../integrate/api-keys/).

## How a scheme is added

A scheme is a **tag** on the verifier: `key` is 1, `webauthn-es256` 2, `api-key` 3. Tags are never
reused. A scheme added later, a different passkey algorithm for example, is one more tag (`DEC-19`).

- **It is a change to aledgent's code, recorded in the plan.** A deployment cannot add a scheme by
  configuration. What a deployment configures is which schemes and relying parties it accepts, within
  those its release knows.
- **The specification does not change.** A new scheme binds one of the kinds the specification already
  names: a key, a device-bound credential, or an issued secret.
- **What the change carries:** the scheme's tag and wire name; the shape of its verifier, which the core
  checks when one joins a set; and its proof's verification at ingress, or, for a scheme the core must
  verify itself, in the core.
- **Existing verifiers are untouched.** Every verifier keeps the tag it was written with, so a book's
  history reads the same after the change.
